LLM QUANTIZATION

How does an LLM survive losing 75% of its bits?

Quantization stores each weight in 4 or 8 bits instead of 16. That makes the model smaller and faster to serve. Plain rounding can break it, though. Here's what goes wrong, and how GPTQ, AWQ and SmoothQuant fix it.

Numbers come from the GPTQ, AWQ and SmoothQuant papers (table cited next to each) and Inference Engineering (P. Kiely, ch. 5.1). Sizes are exact arithmetic. Anything else is labelled illustrative.

01

An LLM is mostly numbers you have to move

Every token the model generates reads every weight from GPU memory. Fewer bits per weight means less memory to hold them and less data to move for each token.

Why it matters

Small errors compound. π rounded to 3.14 is off by 0.05%, but cube it and you're off by 0.15%. Round it to 3 and π³ is 27 instead of 31. An LLM stacks dozens of layers and thousands of tokens on top of every rounding error (example from Inference Engineering, ch. 5.1).

05

Fix 1: give every small group its own scale

An outlier only stretches the grid of its own group. Smaller groups mean more scales to store, but each group's grid fits its own weights snugly.

The same 32 weights at 3 bits. Extra storage assumes a 16-bit scale and a 16-bit zero-point per group. Real models use groups of 128.

In hardware

New number formats bake this in. MXFP8 and MXFP4 share one scale per 32 values; NVFP4 uses one per 16, plus a global scale. The more scales, the less an outlier can hurt.

06

Fix 2: protect the 1% that matters

A few input channels carry huge activations every time, so the weights that multiply them matter most. AWQ's insight: find them by looking at the activations, not the weights.

Illustrative picture of what SmoothQuant and AWQ observe in real LLMs: outliers are ≈100× larger than typical values and stay in the same channels for every token. Judging importance by weight size would miss them.

Keep a few channels in 16-bit, round the rest to 3 bits

The trick

Mixed precision is awkward for GPU kernels. So AWQ keeps everything in 4 bits and instead scales the important channels up before rounding (and their activations down by the same factor). The math is unchanged, but those weights now span more levels, so their relative error shrinks. The per-channel scale comes from average activation size, tuned per layer on a small calibration set. No retraining.

07

Fix 3: cancel the error you just made

GPTQ quantizes a layer one column at a time. After rounding each column, it nudges the not-yet-quantized columns to cancel that column's error on real inputs.

  1. 1

    Run a small calibration set through the layer

    Record its inputs X and build H = 2XᵀX. H captures which input directions are correlated and important.

  2. 2

    Round column 1

    Its rounding error is now fixed forever.

  3. 3

    Spread the error onto columns 2…n

    Update them with H⁻¹ so the layer's output, not each weight, stays as close as possible to the original.

  4. 4

    Repeat to the last column, layer by layer

    OPT-175B took ≈4 GPU hours (GPTQ paper).

08

Activations are harder: move the difficulty

Weight-only quantization saves memory. To also use the fast INT8 or FP8 math units, the activations must be quantized too, and they're full of outliers. SmoothQuant divides activation channel j by sj and multiplies the matching weights by sj. The product is the same, but the outliers move into the weights, which handle them easily.

09

What to quantize, and in which format

Not every part of a model is equally fragile. From least to most sensitive (Inference Engineering, ch. 5.1):

low riskWeights

Linear layers are the bulk of the model and the safest to quantize. Often the first and last layers stay in 16-bit.

some riskActivations

Layer outputs have outliers (section 08). Activation functions themselves are rarely quantized.

moderateKV cache

A big win for long contexts and caching, but errors carry forward to every later token.

high riskAttention

Softmax is sensitive to range, and errors compound across thousands of tokens. Usually kept in high precision.

Why float beats int at the same bits: the exponent spaces levels densely near zero and sparsely far away. That suits bell-shaped weights with a few outliers.

10

The recipe for shipping a quantized model

  1. 1

    Pick the precision from your bottleneck

    Out of memory or slow decode → weight-only 4-bit (AWQ/GPTQ). Compute-bound prefill or high batch → FP8/INT8 weights and activations.

  2. 2

    Choose what to quantize

    Start with linear-layer weights. Add activations and the KV cache only if you need them. Leave attention math, embeddings and the output head in 16-bit.

  3. 3

    Use small groups, never one scale per tensor

    g128 for INT4, or block-scaled formats (MXFP8, NVFP4).

  4. 4

    Calibrate with a smart method

    AWQ or GPTQ for low-bit weights, SmoothQuant for W8A8. Use a few hundred samples that look like your traffic.

  5. 5

    Measure against the original

    Perplexity first, then benchmarks (MMLU etc.), then your own evals. The bar is "difference indistinguishable from run-to-run noise."

  6. 6

    Back off where it hurts

    Quantization is a dial, not a switch. Go FP8 instead of FP4, or quantize fewer components.

11

When to use it, and what to watch out for

✓ Great for
  • Fitting a big model on fewer GPUs, or on a laptop
  • Faster decode (memory-bound), especially at small batch sizes
  • More room for KV cache → bigger batches, longer contexts
  • Large models: the bigger the model, the less each weight matters
Watch out
  • Small models are fragile: every weight carries more of the load
  • 3 bits and below: quality drops fast, even with GPTQ/AWQ
  • Calibration data that doesn't match your domain
  • Speedups need real low-bit kernels (e.g. vLLM, TensorRT-LLM); the file size alone isn't speed
12

Interview follow-ups

Why does 4-bit weight-only quantization speed up decode but not prefill much?

Decode reads every weight for just a few tokens, so it is memory-bandwidth-bound. Moving 4× fewer bytes directly speeds it up. Prefill processes many tokens per weight read, so it's compute-bound, and W4A16 still does 16-bit math after de-quantizing. To speed up prefill you need low-precision math: FP8/INT8 activations as well (W8A8).

Symmetric vs asymmetric quantization?

Symmetric: the levels are centred on zero, and scale = max|w| / (2b−1−1). Asymmetric adds a zero-point so the grid covers [min, max] exactly, which is better for skewed ranges and costs one extra number per group. Weights are often done asymmetrically at 3–4 bits; activations are usually symmetric.

Why do activation outliers hurt more than weight outliers?

Weights are fixed, so you can scale each output channel separately offline. Activation outliers sit in fixed input channels and change per token. A per-tensor activation scale gets stretched by them for every token, and you can't give each input channel its own scale without breaking the fast INT8 matmul. SmoothQuant folds that per-channel scale into the weights instead.

GPTQ vs AWQ: which would you pick?

Both are post-training, weight-only and use calibration data. GPTQ reconstructs each layer's output with second-order (Hessian) updates, which is strong but can overfit the calibration set. AWQ only searches per-channel scales, is cheaper and generalizes well across domains. At 4-bit g128 they're usually close. Evaluate both on your own tasks.

What's the difference between PTQ and QAT?

Post-training quantization converts finished weights using a small calibration set in minutes to hours. Quantization-aware training simulates the low-bit rounding during training so the weights adapt to it. It gives the best quality at very low bits but needs the training pipeline (e.g. GPT-OSS released in MXFP4, Kimi K2 Thinking in INT4). With open weights you usually only have PTQ.

How do you check that a quantized model is "good enough"?

Compare against the original on the same harness: perplexity (cheap, sensitive), public benchmarks, and a product-specific eval. You want differences within run-to-run noise. Check long-context and reasoning tasks too, since errors compound over tokens.

Fewer bits, same model: just tame the outliers.
