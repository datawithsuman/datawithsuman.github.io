1 · What we hoped for

Episode 2.1 · From neurons to features

A neuron for “blue”

The survey starts with the ideal case. Suppose a neuron is large and positive whenever the current token is “blue”, near zero otherwise, and pushing it up or down moves the model’s output towards or away from blue things. Then you can say the neuron is the concept blue p. 22. Early interpretability work hunted for exactly such neurons, and found some.

Two words need pinning down first. A concept is a distinct, reusable unit of information you can recognise across contexts: “a dog”, “the colour red”, “words of praise”. It does not need a human name, and a model may use concepts we have no word for p. 21–22. Notice the test in the “blue” example: high activation and a causal effect. Correlation alone is not enough. That pairing comes back in every definition in this series.

2 · What neurons actually do
polysemantic

Many neurons answer to several unrelated things

A neuron is polysemantic if it fires strongly for two distinct concepts on different inputs, and intervening on it affects behaviour tied to both p. 22. The classic example comes from the vision model InceptionV1: neuron 4e:55 seemed to respond to cat faces, the fronts of cars and cat legs Olah et al. 2020.

Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram.
  1. Show the network cat faces: the neuron fires.
  2. Show it the fronts of cars: it fires too.
  3. Cat legs: it fires again.
  4. A neuron-level label is now misleading. This is why polysemanticity breaks the plan of reading a model one neuron at a time.

Update · my check (11 Nov 2026)

Even this textbook example is being re-argued. One of the original authors has since suggested 4e:55 may respond to a shared “V” shape rather than to cats and cars as concepts (reported in blog coverage, not a peer-reviewed paper; summary). my take That is the survey’s point about concepts in miniature: what counts as “one concept” can depend on whose vocabulary you use.

Why would neurons end up mixed? The survey points to representational limits and superposition: the model stores more concepts than it has neurons p. 22. That gets a full episode of its own (3.2).

3 · A better unit: the feature
feature

Stop asking “which neuron?” and ask “which direction?”

The survey’s definition: a feature for a concept c is a geometric structure φ in the model’s activation space whose activation is predictive of and causally relevant to c p. 23. A single neuron is just the special case where φ happens to line up with one axis. Most often in practice, φ is a direction, but the definition deliberately leaves room for other shapes.

Try it: one neuron vs. one direction

Two neurons, two concepts. Each dot is an input: teal dots are cat inputs, amber dots are car inputs. Rotate the readout direction and count how often it “fires” (projection above 0.5) for each concept. Angle 0° is just reading neuron 1.

That is the whole move in one picture. Both concepts push neuron 1 up, so neuron 1 is polysemantic. But there is a direction that responds to cats and ignores cars. The concept is clean; it just isn’t aligned with any neuron. That is why the field analyses the activation space as a whole, not neurons one at a time p. 22.

4 · Not every feature is a line
directions and manifolds

Some concepts live on a circle

The survey notes that models encode some concepts as linear directions and others as low-dimensional manifolds p. 22. A striking example: in GPT-2 and Mistral 7B, days of the week and months of the year sit on a circle, in order. Mistral 7B and Llama 3 8B use these circles to do date arithmetic such as “two days from Monday is”, and intervening on the circle changes the answer Engels et al. 2025.

Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram.
  1. Project the model’s activations on day-of-week tokens onto two directions and the days fall on a circle, in calendar order.
  2. Ask “two days from Monday is…”. The model starts at Monday’s point on the circle.
  3. Adding days becomes a rotation: two steps around the circle lands on Wednesday.
  4. Move the point to a different angle by intervention and the model’s answer moves with it. The circle is causally used, not just decodable. One direction could not represent this wrap-around.
5 · The caveat to carry forward
not unique

Finding one representation is not finding the representation

The survey’s caveat: a candidate feature you identified may be only one of many internal representations of the concept. Its example is refusal. A single direction in the residual stream can steer a model towards or away from refusing, but that does not mean the direction uniquely or completely captures how refusal is represented. Other causally relevant structures may exist that nobody has found p. 23.

The survey also treats every feature-finding method as an operational approximation shaped by its assumptions: readout methods, sparse decompositions and linear probes each find what they are built to find p. 23. my take Read every “we found the X feature” claim in later episodes as “we found an X feature, by method M”.

6 · Takeaways
  1. A concept is a reusable unit of information; it need not have a human name.
  2. A neuron is polysemantic when it responds to, and causally affects, several unrelated concepts. Many do.
  3. A feature is a shape in activation space (usually a direction) that is both predictive of and causally relevant to a concept.
  4. A concept can be clean as a direction even when no single neuron is.
  5. Some features are not lines (days of the week sit on a circle), and any feature you find may be one representation among several.

1 · The residual stream
read · add · pass on

Episode 2.2 · How a transformer computes: one shared stream

A running total that every layer reads and adds to

Picture one vector per token flowing up through the model. Each attention module and each MLP reads the current vector, computes something, and adds its result back. Nothing is overwritten. The survey calls this the residual stream, “the shared communication channel through which transformer components exchange information” p. 23, following Elhage et al. (2021).

Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram.
  1. The stream starts as the token’s embedding.
  2. Attention reads the stream, gathers information from other tokens, and adds its output back.
  3. The MLP reads the updated stream, transforms it, and adds its output back.
  4. Every layer repeats the pattern. Nothing is erased; each block only adds.
  5. At the top, the unembedding turns the final stream into scores (logits) over the vocabulary.
2 · The precise version

Unroll the layers and the stream becomes a sum

Each layer applies h′ = h + Attn(h) and then h = h′ + MLP(h′). Unroll that recursion and you get the equation below p. 24:

    This is “central to mechanistic interpretability”, says the survey, because it lets us “decompose model computation into additive contributions from individual components” p. 24. The equation leaves out layer normalisation. For a fixed input, layer norm is close to an affine map plus a scalar rescaling, so most of it can be folded into neighbouring weights. That makes the clean sum an approximation, but a good one p. 24.

    3 · Attention moves information between tokens
    where to look · what to move

    Each head decides where to look, then what to copy

    Attention is the only part of a transformer that moves information across token positions Wang et al. 2023, App. J. The survey describes its role as routing, aggregating and composing information across positions p. 24.

      Survey §4.4 attention box p. 24; the QK / OV split is from Elhage et al. (2021), as used in Wang et al. p. 3.

      The QK/OV split is worth keeping in mind for the rest of the series: QK decides where information moves from, OV decides what gets written. A module with several heads simply adds the heads’ writes together, which is another sum the decomposition can exploit p. 24.

      4 · MLPs transform and store
      key → value

      An MLP behaves like a large table of pattern → output memories

      The MLP works on each token separately: up-project, apply a nonlinearity, down-project, add to the stream. Its hidden layer is usually much wider than the stream p. 25. The survey assigns MLPs feature transformation and factual knowledge retrieval p. 24.

      A useful way to read it, from Geva et al. (2021): each row of the first matrix is a key that detects a pattern in the input, and each column of the second is a value that pushes the output towards certain tokens. In a 16-layer model, lower layers’ keys caught shallow patterns (texts ending in the same word), upper layers caught semantic ones (TV shows, time ranges). Each layer’s output was usually a blend of hundreds of memories rather than one winner Geva et al. 2021.

      5 · What this simple picture leaves out
      • Layer norm is folded in as an approximation. When an intervention would change normalisation statistics a lot, the clean additive story drifts p. 24.
      • Modern variants change the parts: grouped-query attention (Llama 3), multi-head latent attention (DeepSeek-V2/V3), gated SwiGLU MLPs, and mixture-of-experts layers where a router picks a few “expert” MLPs per token p. 25. Chapter 12 returns to why MoE routing complicates analysis.
      • The residual stream has no privileged basis: nothing acts on its coordinates one at a time, so its individual dimensions don’t mean anything special. That is why features there are found as directions (Ep 3.1).
      6 · Takeaways
      1. Every block reads the residual stream and adds to it; the stream is a running sum.
      2. Unrolled, the stream is the embedding plus every component’s write. That additivity is what lets us attribute outputs to components.
      3. Attention moves information between tokens: QK chooses where to look, OV chooses what to write.
      4. MLPs transform within a token and act like pattern → output memories.
      5. Layer norm and modern variants make the sum an approximation, but a workable one.

      1 · Mechanism and circuit

      Episode 2.3 · Circuits: wiring components into an explanation

      A circuit is a subgraph that does one job

      The survey uses mechanism for the organisation of activations, features and transformations that together produce a behaviour. A circuit is the usual way to write a mechanism down: for a fixed computation with graph G, a circuit C ⊆ G whose nodes are components (attention layers, MLPs) or features, and whose edges are information flowing between them p. 25.

      Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram.
      1. The survey’s Figure 3 example: the model reads “Dallas is in” and should say “Texas”.
      2. Early MLPs turn “Dallas” into features such as “Southern state”, and “in” into a “state” context.
      3. An attention head combines them into “say something starting with T”.
      4. A later MLP sharpens that into “say …exas”.
      5. Out comes “Texas”. The highlighted nodes and edges are the circuit: a small subgraph of the full computation that carries this one job.

      3 · Worked example · the induction circuit
      two heads, one algorithm

      [A][B] … [A] → predict [B]

      An induction head completes repeated patterns. If token A was followed by B earlier, and A appears again, it predicts B. It cannot do this alone. A previous-token head in an earlier layer first writes “the token before me was …” into every position. The induction head then searches for the position whose previous token matches the current token, attends there, and copies what it finds survey p. 58 Olsson et al. 2022.

        Survey §6.8 induction-head box p. 47.

        Try it: run the circuit by hand

        Pick a sequence (or type your own, separated by spaces). The panel runs an idealised two-head induction circuit on the last token and shows what each head does.

        —

        Try the random-token preset: it works just as well, because the rule does not depend on what A and B are. That is exactly how Olsson et al. define an induction head: by its behaviour on repeated sequences of random tokens Olsson et al. 2022. They report that induction heads appear in a sudden phase change early in training, at the same point the model gains most of its in-context learning ability. The evidence is causal for small attention-only models and correlational for larger ones (more in Chapter 10).

        4 · Worked example · a circuit in the wild
        26 heads, 7 roles

        How GPT-2 small knows to say “Mary”

        “When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to” → Mary. This task is indirect object identification (IOI). Wang et al. found the circuit GPT-2 small uses: 26 attention heads in 7 classes, about 1.1% of all (head, position) pairs. The heads implement a three-step algorithm Wang et al. p. 2–4:

        Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram.
        1. The task: of the two names, output the one that is not the subject of the last clause.
        2. Step 1. Duplicate-token heads (helped by induction and previous-token heads) notice that “John” appeared twice.
        3. Step 2. S-inhibition heads pass that on, steering later heads away from “John”.
        4. Step 3. Name-mover heads attend to the remaining name and copy “Mary” into the output.
        5. The surprise: knock out all the name movers and the circuit still works with only a 5% drop, because “backup” heads step in. Redundancy like this makes circuits hard to pin down completely.

        How well does the circuit explain the model?

        Measured as the logit difference between “Mary” and “John”, the circuit alone reaches about 87% of the full model’s performance (it is off by 0.46 out of 3.56). It passes this faithfulness test, but by the authors’ own account it fails the most demanding completeness tests Wang et al. p. 9.

        5 · Circuits in a production model
        query-level

        Dallas → Texas → Austin

        Query-level methods now trace circuits of features in deployed models. Anthropic’s study of Claude 3.5 Haiku found that, for “the capital of the state containing Dallas is”, the model internally activates Texas features first and then combines them with “capital” to say Austin. The two-hop reasoning happens inside a single forward pass, and you can intervene on the intermediate step Lindsey et al. 2025.

        The authors’ own caveat

        These attribution graphs come from an interpretable replacement model that only approximates the real one. The authors report the method gives satisfying insight on only a minority of the prompts they tried (secondary sources summarise this as about a quarter) Lindsey et al. 2025. The survey adds that a full CLT replacement matches the original model’s top-1 prediction on only about 50% of prompts p. 43.

        6 · Takeaways
        1. A circuit is a subgraph of the computation (components or features as nodes, information flow as edges) that carries one behaviour.
        2. Capability circuits explain a task across a dataset; query-level circuits explain one prompt in terms of features.
        3. The induction circuit is two heads in sequence: a previous-token head, then an induction head that matches and copies. It works on any tokens.
        4. Real circuits (IOI) are larger and redundant: backup heads can take over, so a faithful circuit is not necessarily a complete one.
        5. In practice circuits explain part of a behaviour. The survey puts it at typically 60–80% p. 57, which is why Chapter 7 is about evaluating these claims.
