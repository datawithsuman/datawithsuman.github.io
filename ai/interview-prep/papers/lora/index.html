LoRA: fine-tuning a giant model by training two tiny matrices

Hu, Shen, Wallis, Allen-Zhu, Li, Wang, Wang, Chen · Microsoft · ICLR 2022 · arXiv 2106.09685 · 10 sources, checked 11 Nov 2026
In one breath: fine-tuning normally updates every weight, so each task needs a full copy of the model: 350 GB for GPT-3. LoRA's bet is that the change a task needs is simple, so it can be written as the product of two thin matrices, ΔW = BA, with a rank r as small as 1 to 8. The original weights stay frozen; only A and B are trained. On GPT-3 175B that cuts trainable parameters about 10,000×, training memory about 3× (1.2 TB to 350 GB), and each task's checkpoint from 350 GB to 35 MB, while matching or beating full fine-tuning. Because BA can be added into W after training, it adds no extra delay when the model runs.

0 · The running example

One 175B model, a hundred customers

the paper's own scenario

You run GPT-3 175B and want a version tuned for each of 100 customers: SQL generation for one, chat summaries for another. Full fine-tuning means 100 copies of 350 GB: 35 TB of checkpoints.

With LoRA you keep one 350 GB base model and 100 adapters of about 35 MB each: about 354 GB in total §4.2, footnote 4. We'll follow one weight matrix of this model, the query projection in layer 48, which is 12,288 × 12,288.

1 · The problem
A full copy per task

Full fine-tuning doesn't scale to giant models

Fine-tuning updates every parameter, so "the new model contains as many parameters as in the original model". That was an "inconvenience" for GPT-2 or RoBERTa and becomes "a critical deployment challenge" for GPT-3's 175 billion parameters §1.

Training memory

With Adam, every trainable weight also needs its gradient and two optimiser states. For GPT-3 that's about 1.2 TB of GPU memory just to fine-tune.

Storing and switching

Each task produces a full-size model. Serving many tasks means loading and swapping hundreds of gigabytes.

2 · How it was done before
Adapters and prompts

3 · The core idea
ΔW = BA

The change a task needs is low-rank

Earlier work found that pretrained language models have a low "intrinsic dimension": they can be fine-tuned well even when the update is squeezed into a small random subspace (Aghajanyan et al. 2020). LoRA hypothesises that the weight change during fine-tuning also has a low "intrinsic rank" §4.1. So instead of learning a full d × k update, learn it as the product of a d × r matrix and an r × k matrix:

    Equation 3 §4.1 · hover a term.

    Both paths see the same input x and their outputs are added. Only A and B get gradients. For our 12,288 × 12,288 query matrix with r = 4, that's 2 × 12,288 × 4 = 98,304 trainable numbers instead of about 151 million.

    No extra delay when serving

    After training, compute W = W₀ + BA once and serve it like any other model. To switch tasks, subtract BA and add a different B′A′, a quick operation §4.1. Adapters can't do this, because they add layers rather than change existing ones.

    A generalisation of full fine-tuning

    If you apply LoRA to every matrix and set r to the full rank, you recover roughly what full fine-tuning can express. Adapters, by contrast, converge to a different network as you add parameters, and prefix tuning to one that can't take long inputs.

    4 · Where to put it
    Attention matrices

    Spread a small budget across more matrices

    The paper applies LoRA only to the attention projections (Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo) and freezes the feed-forward layers, for simplicity. With a fixed budget of 18M trainable parameters on GPT-3, they compared which matrices to adapt §7.1:

    Putting the whole budget into Wq or Wk was clearly worse. Adapting more matrices at a lower rank beat adapting one at a higher rank: even rank 4 captures enough. Most later practice adapts every linear layer, feed-forward included.

      • GPT-3: LoRA matched or beat full fine-tuning on all three tasks with 4.7M trainable parameters.

      8 · How low can the rank go?
      Rank 1 can be enough

      The update really is low-rank

      WikiSQL accuracy on GPT-3, adapting Wq and Wv (Table 6)

      "To our surprise, a rank as small as one suffices" for adapting Wq and Wv on these tasks; Wq alone needed a somewhat larger r §7.2. Two analyses back this up:

      • Bigger r doesn't find new directions. The top directions learned with r = 8 are also the top ones learned with r = 64; the extra directions at r = 64 look mostly like noise.
      • ΔW amplifies what's already there. The update boosts directions that exist in W but weren't emphasised by pretraining, and boosts them a lot: by about 21.5× for r = 4 in layer 48 §7.3. Fine-tuning seems to turn up useful features the model already had, rather than learn new ones.

      The authors' own caveat

      They "do not expect a small r to work for every task". If the new task were in a different language from pretraining, retraining everything (LoRA with r = d) could clearly win §7.2, footnote 6.

      9 · Takeaways and caveats
      Where it falls short

      What the paper didn't solve

      • Batching different tasks. If you merge BA into W for speed, one forward pass can only serve one task. Keeping adapters separate allows mixing tasks in a batch, at some cost in speed §4.2.
      • Heuristic choices. Which matrices to adapt and which rank to use were picked by experiment; the paper lists finding a principled way as future work.
      • Narrow tests. The evaluation used classification, text-to-SQL, summarisation and table-to-text tasks, not long instruction tuning or learning new domains.
      • Later evidence of a gap. On harder targets, code and maths with up to 20B tokens of continued pretraining, "LoRA substantially underperforms full finetuning", but it also forgets less of the base model's other skills. Full fine-tuning learned updates of 10–100× higher rank than typical LoRA settings arXiv 2405.09673.

      11 · Key terms

      Words to know

      Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
      Adapting a model by training only a small number of new or selected parameters.
      Rank
      The number of independent directions a matrix can represent. A d × r times r × k product has rank at most r.
      Low-rank decomposition
      Writing a big matrix as the product of two thin ones: ΔW = BA.
      Intrinsic dimension / rank
      The idea that the useful change during fine-tuning lives in a small subspace.
      Adapter
      A small extra layer inserted into the network; adds delay because it runs in sequence.
      Merging
      Adding BA into W₀ after training so the model runs at full speed with no extra layers.
      α / r scaling
      A constant that scales the update so the learning rate needn't be retuned when r changes.
      QLoRA
      LoRA on top of a 4-bit quantised frozen model, to fit fine-tuning on one GPU.

      13 · Say it in an interview

      How to explain it

      The 30-second answer

      "LoRA fine-tunes a large model without touching its weights. It assumes the weight change a task needs is low-rank, so for a frozen matrix W it learns ΔW = BA, where B is d × r and A is r × k with r around 1 to 16. B starts at zero so training starts from the pretrained model, and the update is scaled by α / r. Only A and B are trained, which on GPT-3 cut trainable parameters about 10,000×, training memory about 3× and checkpoints from 350 GB to 35 MB, while matching full fine-tuning. After training you merge BA into W, so there's no extra latency, and you can swap adapters per task."

      The 2-minute walkthrough

      Problem: full fine-tuning needs a full model copy per task and Adam's memory for every weight. Earlier fixes either added latency (adapters) or ate sequence length and were hard to tune (prefix tuning). The idea: the update has low intrinsic rank, so ΔW = BA. Walk through one 12,288 × 12,288 matrix: 151M numbers become 98K at r = 4. Explain the zero init of B, α / r, merging, and which matrices (Wq and Wv at low rank beat one matrix at high rank). Results: matches or beats full fine-tuning on GPT-3. Limits: one task per merged batch, and later evidence that LoRA learns less on hard new domains but forgets less.

      Likely follow-ups

      Why initialise B to zero?

      So BA = 0 at the start, and the model begins exactly as the pretrained one. A is random so gradients for B aren't zero; if both were zero, neither would learn.

      Why does LoRA save memory but not 10,000× memory?

      The trainable parameters and their Adam states shrink by about 10,000×, but the frozen model still has to be in GPU memory for the forward and backward passes. On GPT-3 that's 1.2 TB down to 350 GB, about 3×.

      How do you choose the rank?

      Start small (4–16). The paper found r = 1–4 enough for its tasks, and adapting more matrices at low rank beats one matrix at high rank. Use a larger rank, or full fine-tuning, when the task is far from pretraining, such as a new language or domain.

      Does LoRA add inference latency?

      Not if you merge: W = W₀ + BA is the same shape as W₀. If you keep adapters separate to serve many tasks in one batch, there's a small extra cost, which systems like S-LoRA optimise.

      LoRA or full fine-tuning?

      LoRA for most adaptation, many tasks, or limited GPUs, and it forgets less of the base model's skills. Full fine-tuning when you're teaching a lot of new knowledge, like a new domain or continued pretraining, where later work found LoRA learns noticeably less.

      What is QLoRA?

      LoRA with the frozen base model stored in 4-bit, so the biggest memory cost shrinks too. It made fine-tuning a 65B model possible on a single 48 GB GPU.

      What not to say

      • "LoRA compresses the model." The base model is unchanged; LoRA adds a small update.
      • "LoRA always matches full fine-tuning." It did on the paper's tasks; on hard new domains it can fall short.
      • "LoRA makes inference slower." Merged, it adds nothing.
      • "LoRA cuts training memory 10,000×." That's the trainable parameters; memory fell about 3×.
