Probability Metrics

Log-loss, Brier score, reliability diagrams, ECE and calibration methods · built from Guo et al. (2017), Brier (1950), Murphy (1973) and scikit-learn's docs · checked 28 Oct 2026
In one breath: when decisions use the probability itself (pricing, bidding, risk scores, the cost thresholds from lesson 3), you need probabilities that are right, not just well ranked. Calibration means "of all cases predicted 70%, about 70% happen". Log-loss and the Brier score are proper scoring rules: they're best only when predicted probabilities equal the true ones, so they reward both sharpness and calibration. A reliability diagram and the expected calibration error (ECE) isolate calibration. Key insight: you can wreck calibration without changing AUC at all. Modern deep networks tend to be overconfident, and a one-parameter fix, temperature scaling, usually repairs it.
What you'll be able to do after this: read a reliability diagram, explain why AUC can't detect miscalibration, choose between log-loss, Brier and ECE, and calibrate a model properly.

1 · The problem

"The model ranks perfectly, so why are we losing money on bids?"

An ad platform bids price = P(click) × value of a click. If the model says 4% when the true rate is 2%, every bid is double what it should be. The model can still rank ads perfectly, so its AUC looks great. Ranking metrics can't see this error. You need metrics that check whether the numbers themselves are right.

2 · Building blocks

Calibration and sharpness

Calibration

Predicted probabilities match observed frequencies: P(y = 1 | p̂ = p) = p for every p. Guo et al.: "given 100 predictions, each with confidence of 0.8, we expect that 80 should be correctly classified" Guo et al. 2017, p. 2.

Sharpness (resolution)

Predictions far from the base rate. Always predicting the base rate (say 10%) is perfectly calibrated and useless. Good probabilistic models are both calibrated and sharp.

3 · The naive idea and its trap
The trap

"AUC is 0.92, the probabilities must be fine"

AUC only looks at the order of the scores. Squash or stretch every probability with any monotonic function (make them all twice as confident) and the order, and the AUC, are unchanged, while every probability is now wrong. The lab in chapter 5 proves it: move the temperature and AUC stays fixed at its value while log-loss, Brier and ECE all move.

4 · How it's done today

Two proper scoring rules and one calibration error

    ECE as defined by Guo et al., Eq. 3 p. 3; Brier score: Brier (1950); scikit-learn calls it "strictly proper", achieving "the best score only when the estimated probabilities equal the true ones" docs.

    6 · Mechanism 2
    What's inside the Brier score

    Murphy's decomposition: reliability − resolution + uncertainty

      Murphy (1973), via the Brier score article. scikit-learn's warning follows from it: "A lower Brier loss, for instance, does not necessarily mean a better calibrated model" docs; a sharper model can win on resolution while being worse calibrated. The Brier skill score 1 − BS / BSbase-rate rescales it so that predicting the base rate scores 0.

      7 · Mechanism 3
      Modern networks are overconfident

      Bigger, more accurate, and worse calibrated

      Guo et al. found that "modern neural networks, unlike those from a decade ago, are poorly calibrated": a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100 is more accurate than a 5-layer LeNet, but its average confidence is far above its accuracy. Depth, width, batch normalisation and less weight decay all made calibration worse; their explanation is that networks keep lowering training log-loss by growing more confident after they already classify the training data correctly Guo et al. 2017, §3.

      8 · Mechanism 4
      The fixes

      Platt scaling, isotonic regression, temperature scaling

        Guo et al., Eq. 9: "temperature scaling does not affect the model's accuracy" p. 6.

        MethodWhat it fitsUse when
        Platt scalingA logistic regression on the model's score (2 parameters)Binary classifiers with sigmoid-shaped distortion (SVMs, boosted trees); small validation sets
        Temperature scalingOne parameter on the logitsNeural networks, multi-class; the default first try
        Isotonic regressionAny monotonic step functionLarge validation sets and odd-shaped distortions; can overfit small sets
        Histogram binningOne probability per score binSimple, robust; can change class predictions

        In scikit-learn: CalibratedClassifierCV(method="sigmoid" | "isotonic"), fitted on data the model wasn't trained on. Logistic regression is often well calibrated already: it "is more likely to return well calibrated predictions by itself" thanks to its canonical link scikit-learn calibration docs.

        10 · Takeaways and honest caveats

        What to keep

        • ECE depends on binning (number of bins, equal-width vs equal-count) and is noisy with few samples per bin; it also ignores sharpness. Report it with log-loss or Brier, never alone.
        • Calibrate on held-out data, never the training set, and re-check after data drift.
        • Resampling and class weights break calibration (lesson 3); recalibrate afterwards.
        • Calibration is about groups, not individuals. "70%" being right on average says nothing about any one case, and can hide miscalibration within subgroups (check fairness-relevant segments). my take

        Say it in an interview

        How to explain it

        The 30-second answer

        When decisions use the probability itself, like bidding, pricing or risk thresholds, I check calibration as well as ranking. AUC can't detect miscalibration because it only depends on order. Log-loss and Brier are proper scoring rules that reward both calibration and sharpness; a reliability diagram and ECE isolate calibration. Modern neural networks tend to be overconfident, and temperature scaling, one parameter fitted on validation data, usually fixes that without changing accuracy. For other models I'd use Platt scaling or isotonic regression on held-out data.

        Likely follow-ups

        Log-loss vs Brier: which?

        Both are proper. Log-loss is unbounded and harshly punishes confident mistakes, which suits systems where a 0.1% prediction for a frequent event is catastrophic, and it's what models are trained on. Brier is bounded, gentler, decomposes into reliability, resolution and uncertainty, and is common in forecasting and medicine.

        Can a model have high AUC and poor calibration?

        Yes. Any monotonic distortion of the scores keeps AUC identical while making probabilities wrong. Conversely, predicting the base rate for everyone is perfectly calibrated with AUC 0.5.

        How does temperature scaling work, and why doesn't it change accuracy?

        Divide the logits by a scalar T fitted to minimise validation log-loss. It rescales confidence but keeps the logits' order, so the argmax (and accuracy) is unchanged.

        What's wrong with ECE?

        It depends on the binning scheme, is biased with few samples, ignores sharpness, and only looks at the top-class confidence. Pair it with log-loss or Brier and a reliability diagram.

        What not to say

        • "Softmax outputs are probabilities." They sum to 1, but they're often miscalibrated.
        • "Lower Brier means better calibrated." It also rewards sharpness.
        • "Calibrate on the training set." Use held-out data.
