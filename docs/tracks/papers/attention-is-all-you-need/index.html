Attention Is All You Need

What happens if you remove recurrence and keep only attention?

The paper

Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser, Polosukhin · Google Brain, Google Research, University of Toronto · NeurIPS 2017 · arXiv 1706.03762 · 10 sources, checked 9 Nov 2026

In 2017, the best translation models read a sentence one word at a time with recurrent networks, which made them slow to train and weak at linking distant words. This paper threw recurrence out and built the whole model from attention: every word looks at every other word in one step, weighs how relevant each one is, and mixes in what it needs. Add multiple attention heads, position signals so word order isn't lost, and a stack of identical layers, and you get the Transformer. It set new translation records (28.4 BLEU on English-to-German) at a fraction of the training cost, and its architecture became the base of BERT, GPT and every large language model since.

The problem

One sentence, followed through the whole model

from the paper's own figure

"The Law will never be perfect, but its application should be just."

To translate this well, the model has to work out that "its" means "the Law", nine words earlier. The paper shows two attention heads that do exactly that, with sharp attention from "its" back to "Law" and "application" Fig. 4, p. 14. We'll follow this sentence through every part of the model.

One word at a time

Recurrent models were slow and forgot distant words

In 2017 the best translation systems were recurrent networks (RNNs, usually LSTMs) in an encoder-decoder setup. A recurrent network reads word 1, updates a hidden state, then reads word 2, and so on: each hidden state ht depends on ht−1. The paper names the cost directly: this "inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples" p. 2.

Slow to train

Step 9 can't start until step 8 finishes, so a GPU can't process a sentence's words at the same time. Long sentences make it worse, because memory limits how many sentences you can batch instead.

Weak over long distances

To link "its" back to "Law", the signal has to pass through every word in between. The longer the path, the harder the dependency is to learn p. 6.

Attention already existed, as an add-on: it let the decoder look back at the encoder's states while translating. But "in all but a few cases" it sat on top of a recurrent network p. 2. The recurrence, and its sequential bottleneck, stayed.

The idea

Think of a meeting. In a recurrent model, a message passes from person to person down a line, and it fades on the way. With attention, everyone in the room can hear everyone else directly, and each person decides who to listen to.

Notice: without dividing by √dk, bigger keys make softmax pick one word almost completely, and the head stops learning. That one division is why the paper calls it scaled dot-product attention.

Method

Three families

Recurrent, convolutional, and attention as a helper

ApproachHow it links two wordsThe catch
RNN / LSTM encoder-decoder (Sutskever 2014)Passes a hidden state along the sentenceSequential: no parallelism within a sentence; path length grows with distance
RNN + attention (Bahdanau 2014)The decoder attends to all encoder statesStill recurrent underneath, so still sequential
Convolutional (ByteNet, ConvS2S)Stacks of convolutions over neighbouring wordsParallel, but linking distant words takes many layers: linear in distance for ConvS2S, logarithmic for ByteNet

The Transformer's pitch was to keep the parallelism of convolutions and make every pair of words one step apart, using attention alone p. 2. The paper notes one cost of that: averaging over many positions blurs detail, which multi-head attention is designed to fix.

Scaled dot-product attention

Each word asks a question, every word offers an answer

Each word is turned into three vectors. A query says what this word is looking for. A key says what each word offers. A value is the information a word passes on if it's chosen. For "its", the query asks roughly "which noun do I refer to?", and "Law" has a key that matches well.

    Equation 1 p. 4 · hover a term.

    Why divide by √dk?

    If the parts of a query and key are random with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance dk footnote 4, p. 4. With dk = 64, typical scores are 8 times larger than with dk = 1. Large scores push softmax into a region where one weight is nearly 1, the rest nearly 0, and the gradients become "extremely small". Dividing by √dk brings the variance back to about 1. Without it, the paper notes, dot-product attention did worse than additive attention at large dk.

    With scaling on, "its" puts about half its weight on "Law" and a quarter on "application", whatever the key size. Turn scaling off at dk = 64 and "Law" takes 99.2% of the weight: the head stops learning, because almost no gradient flows to the other words.

    Why dot products rather than a small network?

    Additive attention, which scores pairs with a tiny feed-forward network, has similar theory, but dot-product attention "is much faster and more space-efficient in practice" because it's one big matrix multiplication p. 4.

    Eight views at once

    Several smaller attentions beat one big one

    One attention function averages everything into a single weighted mix, so it can only look one way at a time. The paper instead projects Q, K and V h = 8 times with different learned matrices, runs attention on each in parallel, joins the results and projects them back p. 5.

      With dmodel = 512 and 8 heads, each head works in dk = dv = 64 dimensions, so the total cost is about the same as one full-size head p. 5.

      In our sentence, the paper's visualisations show two heads in layer 5 sharply linking "its" to "Law" and "application", while other heads follow sentence structure: "the heads clearly learned to perform different tasks" Fig. 5, p. 15.

      How many heads? (Table 3, rows A)

      At the same total compute, 1 head scored 24.9 BLEU on the development set, 8 heads 25.8, 16 heads 25.8 and 32 heads 25.4. One head is 0.9 BLEU worse than the best, and too many heads hurts too p. 9.

      Three places attention is used

      • Encoder self-attention: every input word attends to every input word.
      • Decoder self-attention, masked: each output word attends only to earlier output words, so the model can't peek at the answer. Future positions are set to −∞ before the softmax.
      • Encoder-decoder attention: queries come from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder, so each output word can look at the whole input sentence p. 5.
      Putting word order back

      Attention alone can't tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog"

      Attention compares every word with every other word, and nothing in that comparison depends on order. Shuffle the words and each output is the same, just shuffled. So the paper adds a position signal to each word's embedding before the first layer p. 6.

        Section 3.5 p. 6.

        Think of a clock with many hands moving at different speeds. Any moment gets a unique combination of hand positions, and the same gap between two moments always looks the same.

        Did the fixed formula matter? (Table 3, row E)

        Learned position embeddings gave nearly identical results: 25.7 BLEU against 25.8 for sines. The authors kept sines because they "may allow the model to extrapolate to sequence lengths longer than the ones encountered during training" p. 6. That was a hope, not a tested result.

        Encoder and decoder

        Six identical layers on each side

        Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram.
        1. Each input word becomes a 512-number embedding, multiplied by √512, plus its position signal.
        2. The encoder is 6 identical layers. Each has self-attention, then a feed-forward network applied to each word on its own, each wrapped as LayerNorm(x + sublayer(x)).
        3. The decoder is also 6 layers, with a third sublayer. Its self-attention is masked so word i only sees words before i.
        4. Encoder-decoder attention lets every output position look at the encoder's final representation of the whole input.
        5. A linear layer and softmax turn the top decoder output into next-word probabilities. The same weight matrix is shared with both embedding layers.

          Where it falls short

          Where it falls short

          What the paper didn't solve

          • Quadratic cost in length. Comparing every pair of words is fine for sentences, expensive for books. The authors flagged this and suggested restricted, local attention as future work p. 7.
          • Position handling was a hope. The sine encodings were chosen because they "may" extrapolate to longer inputs, but this wasn't tested, and later work found absolute encodings extrapolate poorly.
          • Training stability. Layer norm after each residual (post-LN) needed the careful warm-up. Later work showed that putting the norm before each sublayer (pre-LN) trains more stably, and most modern models do that.
          • Narrow evidence. Results cover two translation pairs and one parsing task, with single runs and no variance reported. BLEU itself is a rough measure of translation quality.
          • A small inconsistency. English-to-French is reported as 41.8 BLEU in the abstract and Table 2, but 41.0 in the text.

          What it changed

          Training could now run in parallel across a whole sequence, and any two words were one step apart. Within two years the same blocks, split into encoder-only and decoder-only models, became BERT and GPT, and the architecture now underlies almost every large language model.

          What to remember. Attention alone, with multiple heads, position signals and residual layers, replaced recurrence. The core equation, softmax(QKT/√dk)V, is still the heart of today's models.

          The base of modern AI

          From a translation model to every large language model

          YearWhat happenedWhich part of the Transformer
          2018GPT: language model pretrainingDecoder only, masked self-attention
          2018BERT: bidirectional pretraining for understandingEncoder only
          2019T5: every text task as text-to-textThe full encoder-decoder

          Today's large language models are mostly decoder-only Transformers with these fixes. The core equation, softmax(QKT/√dk)V, is unchanged. The authors' closing hope, to extend the model to "images, audio and video" p. 10, came true.

          Key terms and sources

          Words to know

          Self-attention
          Each position in a sequence attends to every position in the same sequence.
          Query, key, value
          Three learned projections of each word: what it looks for, what it offers, and what it passes on.
          Scaled dot-product attention
          softmax(QKT/√dk)V. The scaling keeps softmax out of its flat, low-gradient region.
          Multi-head attention
          Several attentions in parallel on smaller projections, joined and mixed.
          Masking
          Setting scores for future positions to −∞ so the decoder can't see words it hasn't produced yet.
          Positional encoding
          A signal added to each embedding so the model knows word order.
          Residual connection and layer norm
          Each sublayer's output is LayerNorm(x + sublayer(x)), which keeps deep stacks trainable.
          BLEU
          A translation score from 0 to 100 based on overlapping word sequences with reference translations.
          Label smoothing
          Training targets that give a little probability to wrong words, which makes the model less overconfident.
