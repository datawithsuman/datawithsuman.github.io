Mechanistic Interpretability 11: Six schools

Which interpretability approach fits which question?

The paper

Barez et al. · Understanding AI from the Inside: The State of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety · arXiv 2611.08438 · 2026 · survey §12 · episodes: 11.1 Different goals, different research programmes, 11.2 Self-explanation, automation, and choosing an approach · checked 11 Nov 2026

A method is a pipeline; an orientation is a research programme with its own goals. The survey names six: pragmatic, ambitious, developmental, across-time, introspective and automated. Each trades understanding, scale and robustness differently, and the right one depends on the question.
pragmaticambitiousdevelopmental / across timeweak spot

The problem

useful now, explanation optional

Episode 11.1 · Different goals, different research programmes

Pick proxy tasks that matter for safety, use any tool

Full mechanistic understanding isn’t required for impact, so work on concrete proxy tasks that can be studied on today’s models, using any method, internal or not p. 102.

  • Evaluation awareness in Claude Sonnet 4.5: the model behaved differently when it detected it was being evaluated. A steering vector that suppresses this awareness let researchers test its actual behaviour, without fully explaining the mechanism p. 102.
  • Auditing games: a model was fine-tuned with a hidden goal, and blinded teams tried to find it, comparing techniques pp. 102–103.

Tools: steering vectors (Ep 9.3), probes (Ch 8), behavioural monitoring (including reading chain-of-thought), prompting as a black-box baseline. Weak spot: these don’t, and aren’t meant to, explain how the behaviour is implemented p. 103.

The idea

The six schools are like different ways to make a city safe. One fixes the worst streets now, one maps every street, one studies how the city grew, and one asks residents what they see. Each answers a different question.

Method

reverse-engineer it all

Only full understanding can rule out hidden failures

Treat interpretability as a science: reverse-engineer the model into a transparent, predictive theory of its algorithms. Make progress by metrics: start loose, improve, tighten. Two example metrics: feature quality (explanations faithful and simulatable) and circuit faithfulness (necessary and sufficient under causal tests like causal scrubbing, with no performance loss) p. 103.

Weak spot: cost. Fine-grained analysis of frontier models is expensive, sometimes intractable, and tools fall far short of the needed coverage. Interpretability-by-design models (Ep 5.3) blur the line: an engineering route to the ambitious goal p. 103.

Explain development, or predict and shape it?

DevelopmentalAcross time
GoalRigorous explanation of how representations, complexity and capabilities developKnowledge of learning that is predictive, causal and precise enough to intervene
Typical questionWhat stages precede a mechanism’s formation?Will this run develop an unsafe behaviour, and how do we stop it?
ToolsSingular learning theory, LLC, checkpoint analysisLongitudinal analysis, forecasting, training interventions (data filtering, gradient routing)

Survey §12.3–12.4 pp. 103–105; the methods themselves are Chapter 10. Example signals for “across time”: activations before fine-tuning predict alignment after it, and emergent-misalignment features sometimes flag a problem before behavioural tests do p. 105.

A common confusion

“Across time” is not against post-training. Post-training is training, and sycophancy and reward hacking mostly arise there. Its criticism is of patching a behaviour without understanding why it developed p. 105.

Episode 11.2 · Self-explanation, automation, and choosing an approach

Just ask the model, then check its answer

Anyone can ask a model “why did you do that?” in a chat window, with no white-box access. The bet rests on privileged access (Ep 3.3): the model may know things about itself that are hard to recover from outside. Models have been fine-tuned to report their reasoning, tendencies, objectives and even training-induced weight changes p. 105.

Failure modeWhat happens
ConfabulationFluent, human-style explanations that don’t reflect the actual computation, e.g. rationalising a memorised answer
MiscalibrationHigh confidence in explanations it can’t check; models can even hallucinate confidently while knowing the right answer
Elicitation sensitivityRephrase the question, get a contradictory answer; a bias towards confirming whatever is suggested
Strategic misreportingSycophantic or deliberately concealing reports when the model has an incentive

Survey §12.5.1 p. 106. Targeted training helps: models trained to disclose hidden objectives or emergent misalignment can report them with high accuracy, so some failures may be limits of current training, not hard limits p. 106.

Validate every self-report by (1) causal interventions: ablate what it says it used; (2) other methods: do probes, SAE features and circuits agree?; (3) paraphrase and resampling consistency; (4) calibration of its stated confidence p. 107. Good for hypotheses, triage and explaining to users; not for claims of absence (“no hidden goal”), formal guarantees, or adversarial settings p. 107.

An explainer agent, a simulator agent, and a score

The classic pipeline (Bills et al.): 1 train an SAE; 2 an investigator LLM reads top-activating texts for a feature and writes a hypothesis; 3 a simulator LLM predicts the feature’s activations on new text from the hypothesis alone, and the correlation with real activations is the explanation’s score; 4 further agents check for deceptive explanations p. 108. Governance risks

  • Error propagation: one explainer’s systematic mistake repeats across every feature it labels.
  • Lost human checks: sanity checks, domain knowledge, scepticism.
  • Automation bias: polished but incomplete or sycophantic reports get over-trusted.
  • Spurious feedback loops: models improved by automated interpretability then used for it can saturate metrics without real understanding.

Survey §12.6.1 pp. 108–109.

Five guardrail layers: (1) blind human review of a random sample (e.g. 5%); (2) consistency across temperatures and explainer models, plus causal spot checks; (3) adversarial tests with backdoored features built to fool the pipeline; (4) calibrated confidence, tests on known ground truth, and reported error rates; (5) regulators treating automated output as hypotheses until validated, with versioning and re-evaluation pp. 109–110. The principle: automation gives breadth, humans give depth and set the criteria (Table 12) p. 110.

What they found

How the schools compare

The survey does not rank the six orientations. It argues they answer different questions, and that the same method, such as SAEs or patching, can serve several of them. The Method tab walks through each one, and the verdict below says how to choose.

Where it falls short

Where each school is weakest

Pragmatic work may help without real understanding. Ambitious reverse-engineering scales badly. Introspective self-reports can confabulate or lie. Automated pipelines propagate their own errors and invite over-trust in polished output.

What it changed

What to remember. Pick the orientation from the question. Pragmatic work helps safety now, ambitious work aims for full understanding, developmental work explains how structure forms, introspection is cheap but least robust to deception, and automated interpretability scales only with human checks.
