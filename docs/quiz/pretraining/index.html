Pretraining

7 questions on Pretraining, from 5 lessons. Pick an answer in your head, then open it.

On the roadmap for: AI Engineering.

  1. Question 1 of 7

    How does one sentence of 8 tokens become 8 training examples?

    1. A. It's copied 8 times
    2. B. At each position the model predicts the next token from everything before it, and causal masking trains all positions in one pass
    3. C. Each word is a separate document
    4. D. It doesn't
    View answer

    ✓ B. At each position the model predicts the next token from everything before it, and causal masking trains all positions in one pass

    Next-token prediction makes every position a target, which is why pretraining is so data-efficient per sequence.

    Lesson: The Next-Token Objective

  2. Question 2 of 7

    Why is deduplication important for pretraining data?

    1. A. To save disk only
    2. B. Repeated text encourages memorisation and wastes compute, and can leak benchmark answers
    3. C. Duplicates improve quality
    4. D. Models can't read duplicates
    View answer

    ✓ B. Repeated text encourages memorisation and wastes compute, and can leak benchmark answers

    Removing near-duplicates improves quality per token and reduces regurgitation.

    Lesson: Pretraining Data: Deduplication, Filtering and Mixtures

  3. Question 3 of 7

    By the Chinchilla scaling result, roughly how many training tokens per parameter is compute-optimal?

    1. A. About 1
    2. B. About 20
    3. C. About 1,000
    4. D. It doesn't matter
    View answer

    ✓ B. About 20

    Earlier models were undertrained. Chinchilla found model size and tokens should grow together, around 20 tokens per parameter.

    Lesson: Scaling Laws: Kaplan vs Chinchilla

  4. Question 4 of 7

    Why was a small model like Llama 3 8B trained on about 15 trillion tokens, far beyond 'compute-optimal'?

    1. A. By mistake
    2. B. Over-training a smaller model makes it better for its size, and it's cheaper to serve for many users
    3. C. Small models need no data
    4. D. To test the GPUs
    View answer

    ✓ B. Over-training a smaller model makes it better for its size, and it's cheaper to serve for many users

    Compute-optimal minimises training cost; when inference dominates total cost, a smaller, longer-trained model wins.

    Lesson: Scaling Laws: Kaplan vs Chinchilla

  5. Question 5 of 7

    Why does training a 70B model with Adam in mixed precision need around a terabyte of memory?

    1. A. The data is large
    2. B. Per parameter you store weights, gradients, an FP32 master copy and two Adam moments, about 16 bytes
    3. C. Activations only
    4. D. The vocabulary is huge
    View answer

    ✓ B. Per parameter you store weights, gradients, an FP32 master copy and two Adam moments, about 16 bytes

    70B × 16 bytes ≈ 1.1 TB before activations, so the model is sharded across many GPUs with FSDP or ZeRO.

    Lesson: Training at Scale: Mixed Precision, Parallelism, ZeRO/FSDP

  6. Question 6 of 7

    What do ZeRO and FSDP do?

    1. A. Compress the data
    2. B. Shard parameters, gradients and optimiser states across GPUs instead of copying them all to each
    3. C. Lower the learning rate
    4. D. Skip the backward pass
    View answer

    ✓ B. Shard parameters, gradients and optimiser states across GPUs instead of copying them all to each

    Each GPU holds only a slice and gathers the rest when needed, cutting memory per GPU.

    Lesson: Training at Scale: Mixed Precision, Parallelism, ZeRO/FSDP

  7. Question 7 of 7

    The loss spikes suddenly at step 40,000. Which is a standard response?

    1. A. Ignore it
    2. B. Roll back to a checkpoint before the spike, skip the bad batches or lower the learning rate, and add stabilisers such as z-loss or QK-norm
    3. C. Double the learning rate
    4. D. Delete the optimiser
    View answer

    ✓ B. Roll back to a checkpoint before the spike, skip the bad batches or lower the learning rate, and add stabilisers such as z-loss or QK-norm

    Spikes often come from exploding logits or attention scores; stabilisers keep them bounded.

    Lesson: Training Instabilities: Loss Spikes, z-loss and QK-norm
