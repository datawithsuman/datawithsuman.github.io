  1. Question 1 of 12

    Two embedding vectors have a cosine similarity of 0. What does that tell you?

    1. A. They point in the same direction, so they mean the same thing
    2. B. They are perpendicular, so they share no direction
    3. C. They point in opposite directions, so their meanings conflict
    4. D. One of them must be the zero vector, so it has no direction
    View answer

    ✓ B. They are perpendicular, so they share no direction

    Cosine similarity is cos θ. It is 0 when θ = 90°, so the vectors are unrelated in direction.

    Lesson: Vectors, Dot Products & Cosine Similarity

  2. Question 2 of 12

    Why does semantic search usually compare embeddings with cosine similarity rather than the raw dot product?

    1. A. Cosine is much cheaper to compute on large embedding tables
    2. B. The dot product can't be negative, so it can't flag unrelated or opposite items
    3. C. Cosine works on raw text, while the dot product needs numbers
    4. D. The dot product also grows with vector length; cosine compares only direction
    View answer

    ✓ D. The dot product also grows with vector length; cosine compares only direction

    a·b = |a||b|cos θ. Long vectors get big dot products even when they mean something different; cosine removes the length.

    Lesson: Vectors, Dot Products & Cosine Similarity

  3. Question 3 of 12

    Why does an L1 penalty push some weights to exactly zero, while L2 only shrinks them?

    1. A. L1 divides each weight by its own size, so the small weights get rounded down to zero
    2. B. L1's constraint has corners on the axes, and its pull doesn't fade as weights shrink
    3. C. L2 only works for neural networks, while L1 works for linear models
    4. D. L1 is applied after training, so it can set leftover weights to zero
    View answer

    ✓ B. L1's constraint has corners on the axes, and its pull doesn't fade as weights shrink

    The L1 diamond touches the loss contours at a corner (a zero weight). L2's pull, 2w, fades as w shrinks, so it rarely reaches zero.

    Lesson: Vector and Matrix Norms

  4. Question 4 of 12

    A square matrix has determinant 0. What does it do to space?

    1. A. It reflects space in a mirror, flipping its orientation
    2. B. It rotates space without changing any lengths or areas
    3. C. It doubles every area, because the determinant measures stretching
    4. D. It squashes space into a lower dimension, so it can't be undone
    View answer

    ✓ D. It squashes space into a lower dimension, so it can't be undone

    The determinant is the factor by which areas or volumes scale. Zero means some direction collapses, so the matrix isn't invertible.

    Lesson: Matrices as Transformations

  5. Question 5 of 12

    A batch X of shape (32, 128) is multiplied by a weight matrix W of shape (128, 64). What is the shape of XW?

    1. A. (32, 64), because the inner dimensions cancel
    2. B. It can't be multiplied, because 32 ≠ 64
    3. C. (128, 128), because the shared dimension is kept
    4. D. (64, 32), because the result is transposed
    View answer

    ✓ A. (32, 64), because the inner dimensions cancel

    Inner dimensions (128) must match and disappear; the outer ones remain: (32, 64).

    Lesson: Matrix Multiplication and Tensor Shapes

  6. Question 6 of 12

    Two features in a linear regression are perfectly correlated. What goes wrong?

    1. A. The model can only predict zero for those rows
    2. B. The R² becomes negative, so the fit is worse than the mean
    3. C. The design matrix loses rank, so the coefficients aren't unique
    4. D. Nothing; least squares simply splits the weight between the two
    View answer

    ✓ C. The design matrix loses rank, so the coefficients aren't unique

    Perfectly correlated columns are linearly dependent, so infinitely many weight combinations give the same fit.

    Lesson: Linear Independence, Basis and Rank

  7. Question 7 of 12

    Geometrically, what are the fitted values ŷ in least-squares regression?

    1. A. The average of the columns of X, weighted by y
    2. B. A rotation of y into the space of the residuals
    3. C. The eigenvectors of XᵀX, scaled by y
    4. D. The projection of y onto the column space of X
    View answer

    ✓ D. The projection of y onto the column space of X

    Least squares picks the point in X's column space closest to y; the residual is orthogonal to that space.

    Lesson: Orthogonality and Projections

  8. Question 8 of 12

    In PCA, what is the first principal component?

    1. A. The single feature with the largest mean value
    2. B. The first column of the data after it has been centred and scaled
    3. C. The covariance matrix's top eigenvector (largest eigenvalue)
    4. D. The direction along which the data varies the least
    View answer

    ✓ C. The covariance matrix's top eigenvector (largest eigenvalue)

    The covariance matrix's top eigenvector is the direction of greatest variance; its eigenvalue is that variance.

    Lesson: Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

  9. Question 9 of 12

    Which statement describes a positive definite matrix A?

    1. A. It has a single repeated eigenvalue
    2. B. xᵀAx > 0 for every non-zero vector x
    3. C. Every entry of A is a positive number
    4. D. Its determinant is exactly 1, so it preserves volume
    View answer

    ✓ B. xᵀAx > 0 for every non-zero vector x

    Positive definite means every direction curves upward, like the Hessian at a strict minimum or a full-rank covariance matrix.

    Lesson: Positive Definite Matrices

  10. Question 10 of 12

    You keep only the top k singular values of a matrix in its SVD. What do you get?

    1. A. The inverse of the matrix, computed more stably than a direct inverse
    2. B. The best rank-k approximation, with the smallest squared error
    3. C. A matrix with only k rows, keeping the largest ones
    4. D. The k largest eigenvalues, ignoring the vectors
    View answer

    ✓ B. The best rank-k approximation, with the smallest squared error

    This is the Eckart–Young result, and the idea behind low-rank compression and LoRA.

    Lesson: SVD & Low-Rank Approximation

  11. Question 11 of 12

    Why shouldn't you compute A⁻¹ to solve Ax = b?

    1. A. Factorising A (LU, QR, Cholesky) is faster and more numerically stable
    2. B. Inverses can only be computed for 2×2 and 3×3 matrices
    3. C. Inverses don't exist for most of the square matrices used in practice
    4. D. The inverse gives a different answer from a factorisation
    View answer

    ✓ A. Factorising A (LU, QR, Cholesky) is faster and more numerically stable

    Forming the inverse does more work and amplifies rounding errors; a factorisation solves the system directly.

    Lesson: Solving Ax = b: LU, QR, Cholesky and the Pseudoinverse

  12. Question 12 of 12

    What is the gradient of the least-squares loss ‖Xw − y‖² with respect to w?

    1. A. 2XᵀXw
    2. B. 2(Xw − y)
    3. C. 2Xᵀ(Xw − y)
    4. D. 2X(Xw − y)ᵀ
    View answer

    ✓ C. 2Xᵀ(Xw − y)

    Chain rule: the derivative of ‖r‖² is 2r, and r = Xw − y brings in Xᵀ. Setting it to zero gives the normal equations.

    Lesson: Matrix Calculus
