  1. Question 1 of 12

    A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 99% of cases and wrongly flags 1% of healthy people. You test positive. Roughly how likely is it that you have the disease?

    1. A. 99%
    2. B. About 50%
    3. C. 1%
    4. D. About 90%
    View answer

    ✓ B. About 50%

    Per 10,000 people: 99 true positives and 99 false positives (1% of 9,900). So 99 / 198 ≈ 50%. The rare base rate matters.

    Lesson: Probability Basics & Bayes' Rule

  2. Question 2 of 12

    What does it mean that X and Y are conditionally independent given Z?

    1. A. X and Y never occur together
    2. B. P(X, Y | Z) = P(X | Z) · P(Y | Z): once you know Z, Y tells you nothing more about X
    3. C. X and Y are independent of Z
    4. D. P(X | Y) = P(Y | X)
    View answer

    ✓ B. P(X, Y | Z) = P(X | Z) · P(Y | Z): once you know Z, Y tells you nothing more about X

    Two things can be correlated overall yet independent once a common cause Z is known. Naive Bayes rests on this.

    Lesson: Joint, Marginal and Conditional Distributions

  3. Question 3 of 12

    You assume the target is Gaussian noise around the model's prediction. Maximising the likelihood is the same as minimising which loss?

    1. A. Cross-entropy
    2. B. Mean squared error
    3. C. Hinge loss
    4. D. Absolute error
    View answer

    ✓ B. Mean squared error

    The Gaussian log-likelihood is −(y − ŷ)²/2σ² plus a constant, so maximising it minimises squared error. A Bernoulli target gives log-loss.

    Lesson: Common Probability Distributions

  4. Question 4 of 12

    You average many models whose errors are positively correlated. What happens to the variance of the average as you add more models?

    1. A. It falls to zero
    2. B. It falls but levels off at a floor set by the correlation
    3. C. It stays the same
    4. D. It increases
    View answer

    ✓ B. It falls but levels off at a floor set by the correlation

    Var = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/n. The second term shrinks with n, but ρσ² stays, so correlated models help less.

    Lesson: Expectation, Variance & Covariance

  5. Question 5 of 12

    What does the central limit theorem say?

    1. A. Every dataset is normally distributed
    2. B. The sample mean of many independent draws is approximately normal, whatever the population's shape (if its variance is finite)
    3. C. The sample mean equals the population mean
    4. D. Large samples have no outliers
    View answer

    ✓ B. The sample mean of many independent draws is approximately normal, whatever the population's shape (if its variance is finite)

    The law of large numbers says the mean converges; the CLT describes the shape of its spread, which is why z-tests and confidence intervals work.

    Lesson: Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem

  6. Question 6 of 12

    By concentration bounds such as Hoeffding's, the error of a test-set estimate shrinks like 1/√n. To halve the error margin, how many test samples do you need?

    1. A. 2×
    2. B. 4×
    3. C. 8×
    4. D. The same
    View answer

    ✓ B. 4×

    Margin ∝ 1/√n, so halving it needs √n to double, which means 4× the samples.

    Lesson: Concentration Inequalities

  7. Question 7 of 12

    L2 regularisation on a model's weights is the same as MAP estimation with which prior?

    1. A. A uniform prior
    2. B. A Gaussian prior centred at zero
    3. C. A Laplace prior
    4. D. No prior
    View answer

    ✓ B. A Gaussian prior centred at zero

    The log of a zero-mean Gaussian prior is −w²/2τ², which adds an L2 penalty. A Laplace prior gives L1.

    Lesson: Maximum Likelihood & MAP

  8. Question 8 of 12

    You use a Beta prior for a coin's probability of heads and observe some flips. Why is this convenient?

    1. A. The posterior is also a Beta, so you just add the counts of heads and tails
    2. B. You no longer need data
    3. C. It always gives probability 0.5
    4. D. It makes the posterior Gaussian
    View answer

    ✓ A. The posterior is also a Beta, so you just add the counts of heads and tails

    Beta is conjugate to the Bernoulli/binomial likelihood: Beta(a, b) becomes Beta(a + heads, b + tails).

    Lesson: Bayesian Inference and Conjugate Priors

  9. Question 9 of 12

    What is the stationary distribution π of a Markov chain with transition matrix P?

    1. A. The starting state
    2. B. A distribution that doesn't change after one more step: π = πP
    3. C. The state visited least
    4. D. The largest entry of P
    View answer

    ✓ B. A distribution that doesn't change after one more step: π = πP

    If the chain is in π, it stays in π. Many chains converge to it from any start, which MCMC relies on.

    Lesson: Markov Chains

  10. Question 10 of 12

    How does importance sampling estimate an expectation under a hard distribution p?

    1. A. It samples from p directly
    2. B. It samples from an easier distribution q and weights each sample by p(x)/q(x)
    3. C. It takes the mode of p
    4. D. It averages p over a grid
    View answer

    ✓ B. It samples from an easier distribution q and weights each sample by p(x)/q(x)

    The weights correct for sampling from the wrong distribution. It works poorly when q misses regions where p is large.

    Lesson: Sampling Methods: Inverse Transform, Importance Sampling and MCMC

  11. Question 11 of 12

    On average it takes 4 flips to see HT, but 6 flips to see HH. Why the difference?

    1. A. HH is less likely than HT
    2. B. After H, a T sends you back to the start when you want HH, but when you want HT an extra H keeps your progress
    3. C. Coins favour tails
    4. D. It's random, the averages are equal
    View answer

    ✓ B. After H, a T sends you back to the start when you want HH, but when you want HT an extra H keeps your progress

    Both pairs have probability 1/4 per position, but a failed HH attempt (HT) wastes your progress, while for HT a failure (HH) leaves you one step away.

    Lesson: Coin Flip Practice

  12. Question 12 of 12

    In the Monty Hall game, you pick a door, the host opens a losing door, and offers a switch. Should you switch?

    1. A. No, it's 50/50
    2. B. Yes, switching wins 2/3 of the time
    3. C. It doesn't matter
    4. D. Only if you picked door 1
    View answer

    ✓ B. Yes, switching wins 2/3 of the time

    Your first pick wins 1/3 of the time. The host's choice concentrates the other 2/3 on the remaining door.

    Lesson: Classic Probability Puzzles
